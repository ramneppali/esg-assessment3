This is the brief on the new climate reporting standards. If you are first-year student learning the fundamentals, here's the deal. Starting in 2025, new Australian and international sustainability reporting standards, AASB S2 and IFRS S2, mandate that large businesses transparently report exactly how climate change affects their bottom line. First, let's look at the core objective. Companies must now disclose physical risks like floods or heat waves, transition risks like new carbon taxes, and opportunities that could reasonably affect their cash flow, access to finance, or cost of capital over the short, medium, or long-term. Think of it as a financial weather report. This isn't corporate charity. It's about whether the business can financially survive a changing climate. Second, how exactly do companies structure this massive amount of information? They'll use four core pillars, governance for who oversees risks, strategy for how the business adapts, risk management for the processes used to spot risks, and metrics and targets for the hard numbers. These pillars sound great on paper, but how do we know companies won't just hide behind vague promises? Finally, we answer that with the strictest required metric, mandatory greenhouse gas tracking. Scope 1 covers direct emissions like company vehicles, Scope 2 is indirect emissions like office electricity, and Scope 3 covers all other indirect emissions across the entire value chain, from suppliers to the end user. Scope 3 is literally like a diet where you have to count your friend's calories too, highlighting just how incredibly comprehensive and challenging this requirement is for businesses. Climate reporting has officially shifted from an optional PR exercise into a strictly regulated pillar of corporate finance.
